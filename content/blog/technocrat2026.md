>About   [**Technocracy**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technocracy) ; [**Public Intellectual**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technocracy)

A technocrat is a technical expert, a scientist, engineer, or economist who holds influence over public policy through specialized knowledge rather than through election or direct accountability to the people affected by that policy. Technocrats favor evidence-based decisions, occupy bureaucratic roles in central banks, treasury departments and advisory boards, and are frequently presented as politically neutral administrators. It is worth asking whether the language, data, methods, goal researchers use when writing about health equity reveals the same instincts.

In PLOS Global Public Health, Koay, Bowleg, Lee and Burgess sarcastically says in their opinion piece, “How (not) to write about health equity,” catalogues the euphemisms that let researchers describe injustice without naming who causes it. Phrases such as “vulnerable populations,” “social determinants of health,” “resilience” and “political will” compress histories of oppression into technical vocabulary. Under the manipulating from the technocratic researcher, this is unmistakable: the evidence-based policy recommendations that performs neutrality, written by people trained to analyses systems rather than to assign responsibility within them.

The same traits that make technocrats valuable — rational planning, data fluency, long-term thinking — are what let research describe “differences” and “disparities” instead of the policy choices that produce them. A technocrat calling for “more research” before acting is indistinguishable from an administrator protecting institutional caution; a researcher praising “intersectoral collaboration” without asking who sits at the table, or who funds it, is practicing the same bureaucratic neutrality that keeps technocrats insulated from democratic accountability.

This is not a flaw unique to any one author or institution. It is what happens when expertise is treated as a substitute for responsibility — when “lived experience” is invited into the room to be thanked, not to decide; when “accountability” itself becomes the one word everyone agrees to avoid. The PLOS authors name this directly: researchers are trained to be impartial, to describe the fire and record its distribution, and to stay quiet about who struck the match.

The question this raises for every researcher, policymaker and health professional is not rhetorical. It is a choice between two roles that can look identical on paper but are not the same in practice: the technocrat, who manages systems efficiently while remaining answerable mainly to funders, institutions and peer review — and the public servant, who accepts that naming power, demanding redistribution, and being judged by the people affected is part of the job, not a departure from it.

Before the next report, roundtable or research proposal, that choice is worth making consciously. The data will still be rigorous. The difference is whether it is written to protect power or to hold it to account.
